Whose jobs, and in what state

Two controls, two questions. My work · My team · All jobs picks whose jobs; Active · Completed · Archived picks what state they are in. Today they sit in different bars in the same visual language, which makes them read as two sets of tabs rather than one narrowing the other — and the counts move when you switch the top one, with nothing on the page explaining why.

Every option below is live. Click the tabs and watch the other control's counts change; that is the relationship each arrangement is trying to make obvious.

Now

As built

The baseline to judge against

What it gets right

Both controls are visible at once, and the state tabs sit directly above the list they filter.

The problem

Nothing marks one as subordinate to the other. Two rows of counted tabs, one above the other, read as peers — so the counts appear to contradict each other rather than to nest.

A

One bar, two groups

Both sets of tabs move into the black bar

Why it might work

One place to look. Side by side on a single line, left to right, they read as a sentence — my work, active — instead of as two competing rows.

What it costs

It puts the state tabs a long way from the list they filter, and the divider is doing all the work of saying these are two questions. A narrow window has to drop one group somewhere.

B

Say the nesting out loud

Smallest change — the toolbar names what it is inside of

Why it might work

Nothing moves. The toolbar simply leads with the view it is sitting inside, so the counts have a stated subject: these three numbers are within My work. One line of markup, and it survives any later change to either control.

What it costs

It explains the relationship rather than showing it, and it repeats the view's name on screen twice. The weakest option if the black bar is what people are skipping past.

C

Both come down to the list

The black bar goes back to being chrome

Why it might work

Everything that narrows the list lives with the list, and the two controls are given different shapes — a soft pill group for whose, the hard outlined segments for state — so they stop looking like the same control twice. The black bar becomes navigation again.

What it costs

The busiest toolbar of the five, and it takes the view switch out of the global bar where a reader might expect account-level things to live.

D

One control, a grid behind it

Click the black button

Why it might work

The two questions are genuinely one choice, so this makes them one control. The grid is the only option here that shows every count at once — you can see that My team has nothing archived without switching to find out.

What it costs

Six visible tabs become one button, so nothing is switchable in a single click any more, and the arrangement has to be opened before it teaches anything. The biggest departure from what people use today.

E

A sentence with two blanks

Both picks inline, in reading order

Why it might work

The grammar carries the relationship, which no arrangement of tabs can do on its own: state qualifies jobs, and jobs belong to someone. Quietest toolbar of the five.

What it costs

Two dropdowns instead of six tabs — slower for someone who switches all day, and prose in a toolbar is a different register from the rest of the product.
